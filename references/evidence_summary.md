# Evidence base for a 3D-printable any-angle stance-control knee joint (KAFO)

Companion file: `references.csv` (137 rows: 118 DOI-resolved via CrossRef, 19 web sources with access date). Citation keys in square brackets below match the `key` column. Numbers quoted inside single quotes are verbatim from the source abstract or full text; numbers without quotes are paraphrased from the abstract. Items marked **[uncertain]** rest on recall, on secondary citation, or on a value I could not read directly, and should be checked against the full text before they go into the manuscript.

## 1. Who needs this joint

### Polio and post-polio

The target population is large and skewed toward low- and middle-income countries. The most-cited global figure is 12-20 million people living with polio sequelae, originating in Gonzalez, Olsson and Borg's Lancet Neurology review [gonzalez2010] and restated by Groce et al., whose abstract reads 'As many as 20 million continue to live with the disabling consequences of the disease' and who note that wild poliovirus fell from 'the estimated 350,000 annual cases in 125 countries in 1988' to 223 cases in 2012 [groce2014]. The same paper makes the point that matters for an LMIC design brief: in high-income countries almost all survivors are now over 50, whereas in countries whose vaccination campaigns reached full coverage only after 1988, 'millions disabled by polio are still children or young adults' [groce2014]. A lower WHO figure of 12 million people with polio-related disability circulates in patient-organisation material [reeve_polio2025]; treat the span 12-20 million as the honest range. Post-polio syndrome (new weakness or fatigability persisting at least one year, diagnosed by exclusion) is defined in [lo2018] and [farbu2006]; cohort prevalence data are in [ragonese2005] (abstract numbers not retrieved **[uncertain]**).

### Spinal cord injury and stroke

GBD 2019 estimates '20.6 million (95% UI 18.9 to 23.6) individuals were living with SCI in 2019' with 0.9 million incident cases per year [gbdsci2023]; regional prevalence ranges from 250 to 906 per million [singh2014]. In the United States, NSCISC reports about 18,000-18,400 new traumatic SCI per year (54 per million) and a prevalent population near 305,000-309,000; incomplete lesions dominate (2024 sheet: incomplete tetraplegia 47.4%, incomplete paraplegia 20.0%; 2025 sheet, since 2015: 37.3% and 32.0%) [nscisc2024; nscisc2025; nscisc2016]. The two sheets differ in how the denominator is defined, so quote one and name it. Incomplete SCI with preserved hip flexion and absent quadriceps is the classic SCKAFO indication.

Stroke supplies the largest knee-instability population in high-income settings. Cooper et al. open with '40% to 60% of stroke patients suffer from knee joint hyperextension during gait' and, in 20 hemiparetic subjects, relate mid-stance hyperextension to plantarflexor weakness (p = 0.044) [cooper2012]. Global stroke prevalence and incidence come from GBD 2021 [gbdstroke2024] (abstract carried methods only; take counts from the paper's Findings **[uncertain]**).

### How many use KAFOs, and the LMIC gap

No direct global count of KAFO users exists in the sources retrieved. The best proxies are: 'More than 1.5 million people in the United States have partial or complete paralysis of the extremities', with assistive-technology use falling with age 'in part, to consumer rejection of KAFO designs' [kafo_biomech_persp2006]; and the UK HTA report, in which 238 health-care professionals described a prescribing landscape where 'approximately half' of knee-instability orthoses are custom-made [hta2016]. WHO's 2017 standards state that 'only 1 in 10 people in need has access to assistive products, including prostheses and orthoses' [who_ispo2017]; ISPO quantifies the need as 0.5% of any population, i.e. 35-40 million people requiring P&O services, with 'two to four times more people' needing orthotic than prosthetic treatment [ispo_pando_day]. In Sierra Leone and Malawi, 222 device users were most dissatisfied with comfort (46%), dimensions (39%) and safety (38%), and 66% (Sierra Leone) versus 42% (Malawi) could not or struggled to walk on uneven ground [magnusson2017]; 49 P&O professionals across four LMICs describe the service constraints [lmic_po_services2019].

## 2. Why the locked knee is the problem

Locking the knee in extension for the whole cycle imposes 'an energy-inefficient long-legged gait pattern (circumduction, vaulting, or hip hiking)' and, over years, drives hip hyperextension, lumbar lordosis, skin shear from pistoning, and ligament stress-deprivation; the same review reports that KAFO uprights and shells 'partially share (range, 30%-83%) the load that transfers through the lower limb' [locked_kafo_iatrogenic2023]. Yakimovich et al. give the standard engineering statement: locked KAFOs produce 'abnormal gait patterns characterized by hip hiking and leg circumduction during swing' [yakimovich2009]. Quantitative definitions of hip hiking and circumduction (23 stroke vs 23 controls) are in [kerrigan2001], and the mechanics of vaulting (contralateral toe walking for clearance) in [kerrigan2000]. The energetic penalty of pathological gait generally is reviewed in [waters1999] (**[uncertain]** on specific KAFO percentages; abstract not retrievable). Even a lighter locked KAFO helps measurably: in 20 polio survivors a carbon-composite KAFO cut energy cost 8% versus the original KAFO and closed 18% of the gap to normative values [brehm2007], at the price of being 50% more expensive while 27.8% lighter [hachisuka2007]. A 2025 EMBC paper on biomechanical consequences of motion-restricting orthoses exists [embc2025_restrict] (abstract not retrieved), and a standardised quantitative gait description of post-polio patients is in [polio_gait2013].

Abandonment data specific to KAFOs are thin but consistent. Across all assistive devices 29.3% were 'completely abandoned', mobility aids most of all, with peaks in the first year and after five years [phillips1993]. In 98 polio survivors fitted with custom KAFOs, 76% were still using them and 24% had discontinued; continued users 'were more often prescribed with a locked rather than a stance-control knee-ankle-foot orthosis (p = 0.015)', a finding the paper attributes to selection (users with less residual function get locked joints) rather than to the SC joint itself [jrm2021_kafo_polio]. The HTA review found only 21 studies (478 participants), all at risk of bias, with individual KAFO costs 'ranging from £73 to £3553' [hta2016]. The older KAFO literature review found 240 articles but only two systematic reviews and two RCTs [fatone2006].

## 3. Stance-control KAFO evidence

### Engineering reviews

The central design review is [yakimovich2009]: it tabulates commercial SCKAFOs and names the recurring limitation, 'dependence on specific joint angles to switch between stance and swing modes'. Its summary table (from the full text, via ResearchGate preview) lists FreeWalk/UTX as 'Locks only in full knee extension', the Fillauer Swing Phase Lock likewise, and 2009 prices: FreeWalk/UTX $2,500 (SCKAFO), Horton $1,350 (joints), SPL $1,880 (joints), Becker E-Knee $4,400, and an electronic unit at US$8,500 **[uncertain on exact column alignment; verify in the PDF]**. Later reviews: 18-article gait and energy review [rafiaei2016]; KAFO state of the art [tian2015]; clinical-benefit synthesis [zacharias2012]; active and passive knee orthoses 1970s-present [knee_orthoses_review2022].

### Clinical trials by device

Horton (cable-released) joint: in 10 users, walking velocity rose in stance-control mode (p < 0.001) but oxygen cost (0.213 vs 0.224 ml/kg/min, p = 0.515) and PCI (p = 0.093) did not change [davis2010; hta2016]; in Zissimopoulos, Fatone and Gard's crossover, hip hiking was eliminated in auto mode yet oxygen cost was identical to locked mode (p > 0.99) [zissimopoulos2007]; McMillan et al. saw lower heart rate in two subjects [mcmillan2004]. The consistent message is that mechanical stance control normalises kinematics far more reliably than it reduces energy cost, which should temper any efficiency claim in the paper.

Mayo electromechanical Dynamic Knee Brace System (Irby, Bernhardt, Kaufman): 21 users (13 experienced, 8 novice); novices gained velocity (55 to 71 cm/s, p = 0.048) and cadence (77 to 85 steps/min); knee ROM 55.2 vs 42.6 degrees; peak knee extension moment 0.29 +/- 0.21 vs 0.087 +/- 0.047 N m/kg [irby2005]. Over six months, 14 users increased peak knee flexion from 49.0 to 55.9 degrees (p = 0.02) and novices increased velocity from 74.7 to 81.2 cm/s [irby2007]; consumer opinions in [bernhardt2006]; the original energy-efficiency case study is [kaufman1996]. Hebert and Liggins report a post-polio case with an automatic SCO [hebert2005]. Hwang et al. evaluated an electromechanical KAFO in polio [hwang2008]; Moreno et al. a controllable KAFO [moreno2008].

Ottobock E-MAG Active and successors: the brochure states that PreLock arms at 15 degrees of flexion and locks at 5 degrees before heel strike, body-weight limit 100 kg, battery about 5000 steps [emag_brochure]; a polio case with 40 years of drop-lock use went from 10.26 degrees swing knee flexion (locked) to 39.98 and then 59.15 degrees after rehabilitation [emag_arm2016]. In 10 E-MAG users switched to the NEURO TRONIC joint, locking failures were 0% for both devices but unlocking failures were 9.9% vs 13.9% of steps under challenging conditions, with net energy cost 3.68 J/kg/m [neurotronic2023]. These unlock-failure rates are the only quantitative reliability benchmark I found for a commercial sensor-based SCKAFO and are a natural target for the new mechanism. The Sensor Lock KAFO is described in [sensorlock2017].

C-Brace (microprocessor hydraulic): stance-phase knee flexion 11 +/- 5.6 degrees, swing flexion 66 vs 74 degrees for SCOs, and all patients managed stairs and ramps step-over-step [schmalz2016]; Orthosis Evaluation Questionnaire improved in ambulation (p = .001), paretic limb health, sounds and well-being [probsting2017]; a 2025 community-ambulator study exists [mpk_kafo2025]. Powered KAFOs in polio improved symmetry of step width, swing time and swing knee flexion but not speed [arazpour2013; arazpour2012].

### Any-angle locking and the SPL failure modes

The pendulum SPL/SPL2 'uses a simple internal pendulum mechanism to lock and unlock the knee depending on the angle of the joint in the sagittal plane', locking 'just prior to heel strike' and unlocking at heel-off; it has two locking positions (0 and 15 degrees) and is mounted on standard 3/4 in (20 mm) sidebars [spl2_manual]. FreeWalk and UTX lock only when the knee reaches full extension and unlock via an ankle cable at roughly 10 degrees dorsiflexion; a user who 'falls short of full knee extension during swing phase' is left unsupported [resna2019_eveleight; utx_capstone]. Becker markets the SafetyStride explicitly as the fix: it 'has the ability of resisting knee flexion at any angle and does not require full 180 degree knee extension in order to lock' [becker_safetystride]. Research any-angle devices: the Ottawa hydraulic joint resisted 69 +/- 4.9 N m and was tested on a 90 kg subject [yakimovich2006; yakimovich2005]; the Ottawa linear-piston angular-velocity device 'engaged within 2 degrees knee flexion and resisted moment loads of over 150 Nm', survived 400,000 cycles, and stopped collapse at peak knee moments 'of up to 235.6 Nm' [lemaire2009]; the Yale quasi-passive compliant SCKAFO [shamaei2014]; a 2025 low-cost solenoid-actuated knee with holding torque of roughly 19 N m per ampere [embc2025solenoid]; and a 2019 RESNA student prototype that used 'three ratchet teeth spaced 10 degrees apart' to tolerate incomplete extension [resna2019_eveleight]. The proposed fine-pitch, phased-twin-pawl ratchet sits between the 10-degree-pitch RESNA design and the continuous hydraulic/piston devices.

## 5. Knee loads for design

### Normal gait

Normative kinematics and kinetics come from Winter's book [winter1991], Kadaba et al. (40 adults) [kadaba1989], Schwartz et al. (speed dependence) [schwartz2008] and the speed regressions of Lelas et al. [lelas2003]. None of the retrieved abstracts states the peak stance-phase knee flexion moment; the commonly quoted range of about 0.4-0.6 N m/kg at self-selected speed is from recall **[uncertain]** and should be computed directly from the Fukuchi or Schreiber datasets (Section 6) for the paper, which also gives speed scaling for free.

### Loads on KAFO joints

The one direct measurement is Bernhardt and Kaufman's case series: a six-axis load cell replaced the lateral knee joint in three quadriceps-weak KAFO users walking at three knee-flexion settings; 'Forces were highest in compression and moments were greatest in the sagittal plane', 'The kinetics did not increase solely with patient weight', and there was 'substantial variability between subjects' [kafo_uprights2011]. The numeric peaks are in the paper's tables (not open access) **[uncertain until read]**. Indirect design points: the Ottawa piston joint was built to resist more than 150 N m and saw 235.6 N m in collapse tests [lemaire2009]; the Ottawa hydraulic joint held 69 N m [yakimovich2006]; and the uprights carry 30-83% of limb load according to the 2023 review [locked_kafo_iatrogenic2023]. A model-based method for estimating leg-orthosis contact forces from gait analysis is [mech_theory_orthosis_force2020]. Peak knee extension moments actually generated by SCO users were 0.087-0.29 N m/kg [irby2005], far below able-bodied values, which supports sizing the lock for a stumble or collapse event rather than for steady gait.

### Standards as a proxy

No ISO structural test exists for orthotic knee joints, so prosthetic standards are the usual surrogate. ISO 10328 cyclic axial test forces are 1234 N (condition I) and 1142 N (condition II) at level P5, 1137/1028 N at P4 and 884/791 N at P3 [iso10328_amd2013]; the ultimate static force for condition II is 4025 N at P5 and 4425 N at P6 [iso10328_sockets2023; socket_iso10328_1999]. ISO 22675 covers ankle-foot devices and ISO 22523 the general requirements for external limb prostheses and orthoses **[uncertain: not fetched; cite standard numbers only]**. Printed prosthetic sockets have been tested to ISO 10328 by two independent centres for a 125 kg user [sockets_strength2020], and a 2022 systematic review pooled failure data for 15 printed and 26 laminated sockets [sockets_sr2022]; Pousett et al. found PLA printed sockets passed the static ultimate test for cushion liners but 'broke circumferentially' in early-stance failure mode [pousett2019]. A paediatric prosthetic knee FEA against ISO 10328:2006 ranked Al 7175 highest [paed_knee_material2015].

### Cycles per year

I found no step-count study specific to KAFO or SCKAFO users. The E-MAG brochure's battery rating of 'about 5000 steps' per charge is a manufacturer's design assumption for a day's use [emag_brochure]. Tudor-Locke and Bassett's public-health indices (sedentary < 5000 steps/day up to active >= 10,000) [tudorlocke2004] **[uncertain: classification from recall]**. A defensible design envelope is 2000-5000 steps/day on the braced limb, i.e. roughly 0.7-1.8 million lock-unlock cycles per year, which is the quantity the paper should state as an assumption. The 400,000-cycle bench test of the Ottawa piston joint [lemaire2009] is the only published orthotic-joint durability number retrieved.

## 6. Public gait datasets

[fukuchi2018]: 42 healthy adults (24 young, 18 older), overground and treadmill, multiple speeds; lower-extremity and pelvis kinematics plus kinetics, raw and processed, c3d and ASCII, on Figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.5722711). Thigh (segment) angle is derivable from the marker data, which is what a pendulum trigger needs. [schreiber2019]: 50 adults, five speed bands (0-0.4, 0.4-0.8, 0.8-1.2 m/s, self-selected, fast), 52 whole-body markers, 3D GRF and moments, EMG, 1143 trials. [camargo2021]: level walking, ramps and stairs with kinematics, kinetics, EMG and wearable sensors (variables per paper). [lencioni2019]: 50 subjects aged 6-72, walking and stair ascent/descent, joint angles in three planes, GRF, moments, powers, CoM and EMG. [moreira2021]: 16 subjects at seven speeds (1.0-4.0 km/h). [reznick2021]: 10 subjects, inclines of 0, 5 and 10 degrees, speeds 0.8-1.2 m/s, stairs 20-35 degrees, running and accelerations. [vanderzee2022]: 33 speed x step-length x step-width conditions (0.7-2.0 m/s) with inverse dynamics. [bovi2011] and [winter1991] are the older multi-task and normative references. Together these cover the slow speeds and inclines where a thigh-angle trigger is most likely to mis-phase.

## 7. 3D-printing materials

### Static properties and anisotropy

FDM parts are weakest across layers: ABS reached 65-72% of injection-moulded tensile strength and 80-90% of compressive strength [ahn2002]; shear modulus and shear yield varied by up to 33% with raster and build orientation in ABS and PC [cantrell2017]; raising the extrusion multiplier closed voids and lifted strength by about 10% in-plane and 50% in the build direction, giving nearly isotropic parts [fff_voids_anis2022]. Commercial filament tensile data are tabulated in [tanikella2017]. PETG property studies: [hsueh2021; szykiedans2017; petg_durgashyam2019; petg_infill2020]. Polycarbonate: characterisation and simulation [pc_fdm2015], creep [pc_creep2018]. Polyamide 12: welding versus anisotropy [pa12_fff_anis2020]. Carbon-fibre polyamide by FFF: short-CF PA12 modulus 1.4-8.8 GPa and tensile strength 40-90 MPa [scf_pa_fff2021]; optimised short-CF polyamide UTS 117.1 +/- 5.7 MPa [cf_pa_fff2022]; earlier CF/PA12 and CF/ABS work in [liao2018; ning2015; tekinalp2014].

### Fatigue

The most useful single source for this design is the 2026 FFF fatigue study: at R = 0.05 the fatigue limits were Nylon 12 7.4 MPa (17% of tensile strength), PC 3.6 MPa (7%), ASA 8 MPa (25%) and PC-ABS 4.7 MPa (15%), with porosity between 4% and 11%; PC's poor ratio was attributed to its higher porosity [nylon12_pc_fatigue2026]. Those percentages imply tensile strengths near 44 MPa for Nylon 12 and 51 MPa for PC (derived, not quoted). PETG tensile and fatigue S-N data are in [petg_fatigue2019] (closed access, numbers not read **[uncertain]**). Raster orientation governs ABS fatigue life (0 and +/-45 degrees outperform 45 and 90 degrees) [ziemian2015]; PLA fatigue versus build orientation, raster angle and mean stress in [afrose2016; ezeh2019]; flexural fatigue of PC in [puigoriol2018]; nylon SLS versus moulded fatigue in [vanhooreweder2013] and polyamide by different routes in [pa_fatigue_fff2020]; review in [fdm_fatigue_review2020]. Design implication: a ratchet tooth loaded in bending must be kept below roughly 5-8 MPa alternating stress for infinite life in PETG/PA12-class FFF parts unless the pawl geometry puts the tooth in compression.

### Wear and printed gears/ratchets

Printed polymer gear wear is strongly process-dependent: parameter optimisation improved wear performance three-fold [zhang2020gears]; nylon spur gear wear and thermal behaviour [nylon_gear_wear2019]; service life of printed gears [tunalioglu2022]; unlubricated surface durability by material and process [gear_durability2024]; AM carbon-reinforced polyamide gears matched conventional PA66 gear fatigue life [cf_pa_gear_fatigue2024]. One conference paper tests a classic 3D-printed ratchet mechanism at 20% and 30% infill [print_ratchet2022] and another assesses a ratchet-pawl finger prosthesis [ratchet_finger2024]; no study of printed ratchet teeth under repeated impact loading was found.

### Printed orthoses and prostheses

Reviews of AM for orthoses and prostheses [chen2016] and 3D-printed AFOs (11 studies) [wojciechowski2019]; early AFO work [telfer2012; mavroidis2011]; low-cost printed hand [zuniga2015]. Failure evidence is almost entirely from sockets (Section 5); I found no published mechanical-failure study of a 3D-printed orthotic knee joint, which is a gap the paper can claim to address.

## 8. Ratchet, pawl and dog-clutch design

Peer-reviewed sources on ratchet tooth design are sparse; the standard treatments are textbooks (Shigley's Mechanical Engineering Design for friction self-locking of inclined surfaces and power screws; Sclater and Chironis, Mechanisms and Mechanical Devices Sourcebook for ratchet geometry) **[uncertain: book references not DOI-verified; add ISBN editions]**. The self-locking criterion for a pawl on a tooth flank is that the flank angle measured from the normal to the pawl force must be smaller than the friction angle, tan^-1(mu); for a release-under-load design the flank is instead given a positive rake so the tooth pushes the pawl out once the holding force is removed. Dog-clutch engagement literature covers the relevant transient: face dog clutch engagement at low mismatch speed [boka2010] and the effect of tooth chamfer angle on shiftability [dogclutch_chamfer2023]. The RESNA prototype [resna2019_eveleight] and the SafetyStride [becker_safetystride] are the only orthotic examples of multi-position ratchets found.

## 9. Low-cost components for LMICs

The ReMotion Knee (D-Rev) is the reference low-cost polycentric prosthetic knee designed for emerging markets [hamner2013], with a 2024 gait comparison against the Ottobock 3R60 and Ossur Rheo [lowcost_knee_gait2024]. Andrysek's review of 106 articles on developing-world prosthetics [andrysek2010] and his automatic stance-phase-lock prosthetic knee (walking 0.14 m/s faster than low-end knees, PCI unchanged) [andrysek2011] are the closest prosthetic analogues of a passive stance lock. The MIT passive knee for India cites '230,000 above-knee amputees' in need [arelekatti2015]; the Jaipur foot is [sethi1978]. No comparable low-cost stance-control orthotic knee was found in the indexed literature, so the cost positioning must be made against the commercial prices in Section 4.

## Thin or missing evidence

Quantitative joint-load peaks for KAFO knee joints (only one three-subject case series, numbers behind paywall); steps per day in KAFO users (none); normal peak knee moment in N m/kg (needs computing from the datasets); KAFO-specific abandonment beyond one polio cohort; peer-reviewed ratchet tooth design; fatigue data for PETG by FFF; mechanical failure of printed orthotic joints; global KAFO user counts; UTX list price.
